Frequently asked questions

1. How are the parameters of the 3 characteristics that evaluate how optimal a landing site is determined?

  1. Power (Energy): 100% takes as a reference the case in which the solar panels are illuminated by the Sun throughout the entire duration of the mission, also assuming that the panels are in good condition and in the proper position. 100% represents continuous solar illumination—that is, without obstruction from local terrain shadows throughout the mission. This parameter assumes that the panels have an optimal angle of incidence and operate within their nominal thermal range.
  2. Communication: 100% effective communication is considered to be when the spacecraft (lander) does not require relay satellites to communicate with Earth.
  3. Terrain: An ideal terrain is considered to be one that has a mostly flat surface, without significant relief features that could jeopardize the landing.

2. How does the lack of an atmosphere affect horizon and solar measurements on the Moon?

Since there is no atmosphere to refract light, solar rays travel in a straight line. This causes the observed solar elevation angle to be equal to the geometric angle—without the small extra margin of visibility caused by atmospheric refraction on Earth—and produces extremely sharp shadows with no twilight, requiring the horizon to be calculated using pure topographic geometry.

3. How does azimuth affect solar panels and landing?

Throughout a lunar day, the Sun's azimuth changes continuously depending on the location of the landing site. To optimize the capture of available sunlight, the solar panels must be able to track the solar path according to the location. Furthermore, knowing this trajectory allows engineers to calculate the ideal orientation angle at which the lander should touch down to prevent its own structure from casting shadows on the panels or blocking its antenna's line of sight to Earth.

4. Why is the Moon used as a reference frame for calculations instead of Earth?

Because the conditions on the Moon and Earth are completely different; to name a few: a lunar day lasts almost an Earth month, the Moon has no atmosphere to bend light, its gravity is much lower, etc. Using the Moon's own position and physical constants is essential to accurately calculate where the Sun will be, whether there will be a direct line of communication with Earth, and how much actual energy the panels will receive at each coordinate.
